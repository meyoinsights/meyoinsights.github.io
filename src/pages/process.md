Investing is the study of businesses, industries and the prices we pay to own them. Meyo Insights is a public record of that work.

We explore companies, industries, market developments and investment ideas. Sometimes an annual report draws our attention. Sometimes it is an industry shift, an unusual valuation or a contradiction in the published numbers. The work does not need to begin with a neatly framed question. We follow the evidence to see what is worth understanding.

Our aim is to develop sound investment judgment: to distinguish a good business from a good investment, understand the risks, and recognize when the facts call for a different view.

This is the approach we are building. It guides our work; it is not a claim that every investigation has completed every step.

## 1. Find something worth understanding

Our research can begin with a listed company, a promising industry, a new policy, a market cycle, or a lesson from investment history.

We take a broad view. Kenya and East Africa are our starting point, but the same principles apply to businesses elsewhere. A study of an industry may be valuable even if no individual company offers an attractive investment. A company review may reveal that the most important subject is regulation, technology or capital allocation.

We identify the subject, establish its context and decide which evidence can improve our understanding. The focus may change as the research develops.

## 2. Build a reliable evidence base

We read broadly, but give particular weight to original documents.

For companies, these include audited annual reports, financial-statement notes, stock-exchange disclosures, regulatory filings and other primary records. For industries, we use official statistics, regulator publications, company disclosures, specialist studies, whitepapers, academic work and credible reporting.

Each source serves a different purpose. A newspaper article may bring an issue to our attention. An industry report may explain market structure. A management presentation shows what management claims, not necessarily what independent evidence establishes.

We record the publisher, date, reporting period, relevant figures and limitations. We compare competing accounts instead of choosing whichever supports a convenient conclusion. Where important information cannot be verified, we say so.

## 3. Understand the business and its industry

Before estimating value, we study the economics.

Who are the customers? What do they pay for? What drives sales, costs and margins? How much capital is required to operate and grow? What advantages, if any, help the business earn returns over time?

We also examine competitors, suppliers, regulations, customer behaviour and the industry's place in the wider economy. We want to know where bargaining power lies, what determines demand, and how those forces may change.

We pay attention to management's allocation of capital. A business can generate attractive profits and still disappoint shareholders if it repeatedly reinvests them poorly.

The objective is to understand the engine of the business, not just the numbers it reports.

## 4. Read the accounts carefully

Reported profit is only one part of a company's financial story.

We read the income statement, balance sheet and cash-flow statement together, including the auditor's opinion and the notes. We examine revenue, margins, debt, working capital, cash generation, capital expenditure and returns on capital.

Our full non-financial company research standard aims for at least five years of history where comparable statements are available. A shorter or incomplete series does not become complete simply because a table needs filling.

Accounting differences matter. Original results may have been restated. Figures for a parent company may not be comparable with consolidated group figures. A condensed announcement may omit important notes. We distinguish these reporting bases and document unresolved differences.

We separate figures reported by a source, calculations derived from them, assumptions and forecasts. Important numbers should carry dates, units and a traceable basis.

The central concern is whether the business can turn its economic position into sustainable cash available to its owners after necessary investment and obligations.

## 5. Separate business quality, value and price

A strong company is not automatically an attractive investment.

A business may have durable advantages, growing profits and excellent management, yet offer poor prospective returns at an excessive price. Another may appear cheap because the market expects serious risks or weak future economics.

We therefore distinguish the quality of a business from its likely value and the price investors are asked to pay.

Where reliable inputs allow it, we consider the value of assets and the cash a business might generate over time. We examine assumptions for growth, reinvestment, profitability and discount rates. We compare more than one plausible outcome and consider the margin of safety between estimated value and market price.

Valuation is an estimate, not a fact. If critical data such as comparable cash flows, share count or material obligations are unresolved, we withhold a confident figure rather than manufacture precision.

## 6. Account for risk and cycles

Conditions change. Businesses do not operate outside economic cycles, technology shifts, regulation or competition.

We examine how a company might perform under less favourable conditions. We look at debt, refinancing, customer concentration, governance, regulatory exposure, capital commitments and industry-specific threats.

We also consider the strongest case against our interpretation. A good analysis should explain not only why an investment could succeed, but also why a reasonable investor might disagree.

We identify the evidence that would change our view. That might be weaker cash conversion, a competitor's entry, higher maintenance costs, a regulatory change or evidence that an earlier assumption was wrong.

Conviction should grow from the evidence, not from repeating a thesis.

## 7. Explain what we found

Our writing should let readers follow the reasoning and judge it for themselves.

We use plain language, explain necessary financial terms and show calculations where they matter. We distinguish reported facts from estimates and opinion. We name the uncertainties rather than hiding them behind a confident conclusion.

Long-form company studies follow a detailed research standard covering the business, its financial position, valuation, industry, future risks, conclusions and sources. Industry reports, investing-history pieces and learning notes may use a different structure suited to their subject.

The result may be an investment view, a company or industry explanation, or simply a useful lesson. Not every investigation ends with a buy or sell conclusion.

## 8. Review, publish and revisit

We use software and AI to organise sources, compare figures, test calculations, draft outlines and challenge our reasoning. Those tools can help us work, but they cannot take responsibility for the conclusions. Important claims are checked against their sources.

Research materials and drafts pass through a private review process. An editorial approval does not automatically authorize publication. Only writing deliberately approved for public release belongs on this website. Posts prepared for X are reviewed separately.

The work does not end with publication. We may discover a mistake, receive better data or change our interpretation. When that happens, we aim to correct the record clearly.

Over time, this journal should show not only what we thought, but how our understanding developed.

**The pursuit of better investment judgment is a continuing practice, not a destination.**
