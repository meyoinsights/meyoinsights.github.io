Kenya Pipeline Company (KPC) became a publicly traded business in March 2026. For the first time, ordinary investors could buy shares in infrastructure that helps move imported petroleum from Mombasa to Kenya's inland markets and neighbouring countries.

The business has appeal. Pipelines and storage terminals are expensive to build, difficult to replicate and essential to the petroleum supply chain. KPC's latest results also report growing revenue and profit.

But **an important distinction sits between a useful business and a sound investment: what are its sustainable earnings, and what price should an owner pay for them?**

KPC's published financial information gives us a reason to be careful. The five-year figures in its IPO document, its earlier audited accounts and the newer consolidated results are not all on the same basis. Some apparently comparable profit figures differ materially.

That does not establish wrongdoing. It does mean that an investor should not choose whichever number produces the most attractive valuation.

## The business behind the shares

KPC earns most of its revenue from **transporting petroleum products**, with storage and related services contributing the remainder. Its infrastructure links the port of Mombasa to inland consumption and regional transit markets.

This is not the same as buying an oil producer. KPC's economics depend more directly on transport volumes, tariffs, operating costs, system reliability and capital investment than on simply predicting the global oil price. Petroleum demand still matters, of course: changes in consumption and the way fuel is distributed affect throughput.

The infrastructure has a potential advantage. Replicating an established pipeline network requires substantial capital, approvals and time. But that does not give KPC unlimited pricing power. Tariff decisions, its public-service role and the economics of its customers constrain what it can charge.

The company also owns Kenya Petroleum Refineries Limited (KPRL), acquired from the National Treasury in October 2023. KPRL's storage facilities add to the operating footprint, while its acquisition complicates comparisons between group and parent-company statements. The IPO memorandum describes the transaction as a common-control business combination with retrospective presentation.

The question is not whether the network matters. It plainly does. The harder question is **how much economic value the network can produce for shareholders after its upkeep, financing and other obligations**.

## Five years of reported performance — on one defined basis

The IPO information memorandum contains a five-year historical compilation for the **parent company alone**. The figures below come from its company statement of profit or loss, for financial years ending 30 June.

| Financial year | Revenue (KES bn) | Profit / (loss) after tax (KES bn) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| FY2021 | 27.99 | −1.74 |
| FY2022 | 26.21 | 4.24 |
| FY2023 | 30.86 | 3.30 |
| FY2024 | 35.37 | 7.41 |
| FY2025 | 38.59 | 8.48 |

*Source: [KPC IPO information memorandum](https://kenyapipelineipo.co.ke/documents/IM.pdf), reporting accountant's compiled company statements (prospectus pp. 150–151). Amounts are converted from KES thousands to billions and rounded to two decimal places. These are **compiled company-only** figures, not a five-year consolidated series.*

On that presentation, revenue in FY2025 was above FY2021, and the parent company moved from a reported loss to a profit. The path was uneven: revenue declined in FY2022, profit fell in FY2023, and both increased later.

It would be tempting to calculate a clean five-year profit growth rate and extend it into the future. We should resist that temptation.

The IPO's reporting accountant, PwC, explains that the historical information was **compiled** from audited statements and management information. A compilation is not a fresh audit or independent review of the compiled figures. The document includes adjustments and reconciliations to the earlier accounts.

That distinction matters. An attractive trend can reflect business improvement, changes to accounting presentation, or both. We need to understand the bridge before treating the series as a fully comparable earnings history.

## The latest results look stronger — but use a different scope

On 14 October 2026, KPC released condensed results for the year ended 30 June 2026. The **consolidated group** reported:

| Group measure | FY2025 comparative | FY2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Total revenue | KES 39.50bn | KES 42.98bn |
| Transport revenue | KES 38.59bn | KES 41.82bn |
| Profit after tax | KES 10.24bn | KES 11.58bn |

*Source: [KPC's FY2026 results announcement](https://kpc.co.ke/wpfd_file/consolidated-audited-financial-results-fy-2025-26-2/), published 14 October 2026, with figures also reported by [Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/corporate/companies/how-kpc-owners-will-share-sh5-8bn-as-profits-rise-13pc-5631744). Figures rounded to two decimal places. The FY2025 column is the comparative presented in the FY2026 release; it is **not** the FY2025 company-only compilation above.*

Those figures imply approximately **9% revenue growth** and **13% profit growth** on the comparative basis used in the FY2026 announcement. Transport revenue makes up almost all of reported group revenue.

For a shareholder, rising throughput and earnings are encouraging. But the condensed release cannot answer every question about maintenance capital spending, provisions, cash conversion, or the complete audit findings. We still need the full underlying notes.

## One year, different profit figures

Here is the most instructive issue in the research.

The IPO's **compiled consolidated** statement reports **KES 7.49 billion** profit after tax for FY2025. Yet KPC's FY2026 condensed release uses a FY2025 group comparative of approximately **KES 10.24 billion**.

That is roughly **KES 2.75 billion apart**, even though the figures refer to the nominal FY2025 financial year.

The comparison is not proof that either statement is false. The IPO's compilation contains adjustments, and accounts can change through restatements, different accounting treatments, or later presentation. KPRL's inclusion in group reporting is another important point of context. But none of these general explanations is, by itself, a completed reconciliation of this particular difference.

The IPO contains a summary of adjustments to the previously audited statements. Our next job is to trace the relevant profit figures from the original FY2025 audit through those adjustments and into the FY2026 comparative. Until that bridge is independently checked, **we should keep the figures in their separate series**.

Why does this matter?

If someone divides FY2026 profit of KES 11.58 billion by the IPO's FY2025 compiled group profit of KES 7.49 billion, the result suggests growth of about **55%**. Using the FY2025 comparative of KES 10.24 billion printed beside FY2026 profit produces about **13%** instead.

The first calculation mixes reporting bases. It creates a much more exciting growth story without establishing that it is the right comparison.

This is exactly the sort of mistake investment research should prevent.

## A public listing changes the ownership question

KPC's January 2026 IPO offered **65% of its issued ordinary shares at KES 9 per share**. It was an *offer for sale* by the Government of Kenya, not a primary share issue that automatically placed the sale proceeds in KPC's own bank account.

The distinction matters. A company may gain access to public capital markets through a listing, but an existing shareholder selling shares is not the same thing as the company raising fresh expansion capital.

Ownership has also continued to evolve. On 21 October 2026, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/nigerias-dangote-buys-25-stake-kenyas-oil-pipeline-network-2026-10-21/) that Aliko Dangote's group had bought a roughly **25% stake** in KPC. A large industry shareholder could bring commercial opportunities, but it also raises ordinary governance questions about influence, strategy and transactions with related businesses. The reported transaction should be checked against updated shareholder disclosures when assessing control.

None of those developments, by themselves, establishes the right price for KPC stock.

## What could go right — and what could go wrong

The strongest case for KPC rests on its established infrastructure, the importance of petroleum logistics to regional trade, and opportunities to transport and store larger volumes. Investment in capacity or neighbouring markets could strengthen the business if it earns an adequate return on the money spent.

The opposing case deserves equal attention.

**Capital expenditure:** Pipelines require maintenance, replacement, safety investment and sometimes costly expansion. Accounting profit is not the amount shareholders can necessarily withdraw.

**Regulation and tariffs:** A strategically important network may face limits on pricing and commercial freedom. More volume does not guarantee better margins if costs or tariff arrangements change.

**Environmental and operational risk:** Spills, interruptions, litigation and remediation can create substantial liabilities. The IPO memorandum identifies these risks directly.

**Governance and accounting:** The history of adjustments, subsidiary consolidation, public ownership and changing shareholders makes careful reading of the audit notes especially important.

**Long-term demand:** Petroleum remains important to the region, but future energy use, competition from other transport systems and infrastructure policy can change the economics of a long-lived asset.

A good investment thesis has to survive all five questions, not just the observation that the pipes are valuable.

## What we still need before valuing the shares

Our next phase is a full reconciliation of the original FY2021–FY2025 accounts, the IPO adjustments and FY2026's comparative disclosures. We also need the complete FY2026 auditor's report and notes before claiming to have reviewed all material provisions and commitments.

Then we can examine cash from operations, the difference between maintenance and expansion spending, financing at both the parent and subsidiary levels, and the earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders. A valuation also requires a verified share count and a market price from a specific trading date.

Only then does it make sense to estimate a range of values, ask what assumptions are already embedded in the share price, and test the margin of safety.

That work may lead to an attractive investment case. It may not. We are not ready to make that call here.

## The lesson from KPC

KPC is a useful first company to study because it combines valuable infrastructure, growing reported earnings, a recent public listing and a set of financial disclosures that reward close reading.

The interesting question is not just **"How much profit did KPC announce?"**

It is whether those profits are comparable, whether they become sustainable cash for owners, and whether the price of ownership leaves room for things to go wrong.

**The business can be important without its shares being cheap. The accounts can be legitimate without every headline comparison being meaningful.**

Good investment judgment starts with making those distinctions.
